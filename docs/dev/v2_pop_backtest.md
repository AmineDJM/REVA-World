## Design

| | primary (leak-free) | literal reading of the brief |
|---|---|---|
| jump-off population | observed 1 January **2013** (demo_pjan) | observed 1 January **2014** |
| mortality, fertility, migration from | calendar years **2008–2012** | calendar years **2009–2013** |
| target | observed 1 January 2023 (10 projected years) | observed 1 January 2023 (9 years) |

The brief says "start from observed 2013 population and 2009–2013 rates". Rates for calendar year
2013 need the 2013 deaths/births and the population of 1 January 2014 (exposure and the migration
residual), which lie *after* a 1 January 2013 jump-off; using them would leak the first projection
year. Decision (owner asleep, conservative): the **primary** design ends the base period before the
jump-off (2008–2012 → jump-off 2013); the literal design is reported too, with its jump-off moved to
1 January 2014 so that it is also leak-free. Both are enforced by the model (`project()` refuses a
base period or migration window that does not end before the jump-off year).

Variants: mortality and fertility always constant at base-period rates; net migration either the
**average residual** of the base period (the L1 default; residual years spanning a series break,
i.e. a `b` flag on the following 1 January population, are excluded and listed) or **zero**; the
average residual *including* the break years is reported for comparison. EUROPOP2023 assumptions do
not exist for 2013 and are not used in the back-test.

Benchmarks on the total population (all equally information-free, using 1 January populations up to
the jump-off only):
* **naive no-change**: the 2023 total equals the jump-off total;
* **constant growth**: `P(J) × (P(J)/P(B))^((2023−J)/(J−B))`, the average annual growth between
  1 January of the first base-period year B and the jump-off J, compounded (primary design:
  `P(2013) × (P(2013)/P(2008))²`);
* **linear trend**: `P(J) + (P(J) − P(B)) × (2023−J)/(J−B)`.

The cohort model adds age structure, but the headline comparison is on totals, where constant growth
is the harder benchmark.

Metrics (percent): **APE** of a group total = |projected − observed| / observed × 100 (both sexes);
**MAPE** = mean of the APE over the single-age × sex cells of the group (cells with an observed 0
are excluded and counted). Groups 0–14, 15–64, 65+ (65 to the open age) and all ages. The open
age is the data's, taken from the base-period and jump-off tables only (no structural information
from the target year); if the 2023 table's open group started lower, the projection would be summed
above it for scoring (`scoring_open_age`; never split). In the current vintage no country needs this.

Data vintage: the **current** Eurostat vintage (fetched 2026-10-15), i.e. revised historical data,
not what was published in 2013 (`revised_data_retrospective`). The 2013 jump-off population and the
2008–2012 residuals may embed later intercensal back-revisions, which can make the errors smaller
than a real-time forecaster would have seen. Status flags in the test period
(b = break, p = provisional, e = estimated) are listed per country; a break means the 2023 figure is
not on the same basis as the 2013 one, and part of the error is then a statistical revision, not a
projection error.

## Reading the results honestly

* **Constant growth beats the cohort model on the total in most countries.** In the primary design
  the average-residual projection (breaks excluded) beats the naive no-change total in 23 of 31
  countries but the constant-growth extrapolation in only 13 of 31 (linear trend: 11 of 31). With the
  1.0.0 behaviour (breaks included) it beat constant growth in 11 of 31, i.e. **constant growth was
  better in 20 of 31 countries** (the review's figure); median / mean total APE: cohort model
  2.85 / 4.87 % (breaks excluded; 2.67 / 5.01 % included), constant growth 2.53 / 4.36 %, linear trend
  2.62 / 4.38 %, no-change 5.56 / 6.79 %. Literal design: 11 of 31 against constant growth (2.82 / 4.44
  vs 2.15 / 3.75 %). On population totals ten years ahead the method is **not better** than an equally
  information-free trend; its value is the age structure (65+ group APE median 2.7 %), which the
  trend benchmarks do not provide and which was not benchmarked here.
* It is worse than no-change in Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia, Portugal, Italy, Slovakia, Cyprus and
  Germany (8 countries; Germany 5.2 % vs 3.1 % with the break excluded, 8.9 % before).
* **Excluding series breaks is not a uniform improvement.** Germany (8.90 → 5.23 %), Belgium
  (2.18 → 0.78 %), Greece, Hungary and France improve; Poland (0.13 → 1.22 %), Luxembourg
  (2.57 → 3.59 %) and Switzerland (0.93 → 0.96 %) get worse. Medians move from 2.67 to 2.85 %, means
  from 5.01 to 4.87 %. The exclusion is kept because a census revision is not migration and must not
  be projected forward (H1), not because it fits the back-test better; Poland's 2009 residual
  (−160 k, `b` on 1 January 2010) happened to offset other errors.
* **The migration assumption dominates the error.** The residual-method average of 2008–2012
  captures the migration regime of the financial-crisis years (large emigration from the Baltic
  states and Portugal, which later reversed; low immigration to Germany before 2015). Census
  revisions flagged `b` in the base period (BEL, CHE, DEU, FRA, GRC, HUN, LUX, POL) are now excluded
  and listed per country ("break years excluded"); Germany's 2010 residual (1 January 2010 →
  1 January 2011, the census-based break) is −1.39 million persons against −0.07 to +0.41 million in
  the other base years. Unflagged revisions are not detected by the flags; the run warning
  `migration_residual_outlier` lists suspicious remaining years without excluding them.
* Zero migration is better than average-residual migration in 9 of 31 countries (primary design:
  CYP, EST, IRL, ISL, ITA, LTU, LVA, PRT, ROU; Germany is no longer among them once its census break is
  excluded); neither assumption is a forecast, which is why L2 reports the whole assumption grid.
* Ages 0–14 carry the largest group errors (median APE 7.2 %): births depend on fertility held at
  its 2008–2012 level, while fertility fell in most countries after 2016, and on migration of young
  families. The 65+ group is the most stable (median APE 2.7 %), because it depends mostly on
  survival of cohorts already present.
* Small populations (ISL, MLT, LUX, CYP, LIE) have noisy single-age cells (high single-age MAPE) and
  migration regimes that changed strongly after 2013 (Malta: 421 464 → 542 051 persons, +28.6 %).
